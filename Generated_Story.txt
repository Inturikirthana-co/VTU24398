The rain didn’t just fall in this city; it felt like a grudge. It was greasy and relentless, washing the grime into the gutters until the whole town smelled like stale whiskey and regret. I stood under the awning of The Velvet Cage—a dive that looked like it had lost a fistfight with an industrial mop—and waited for the kind of trouble that always smells faintly of jasmine perfume and cheap gin.

My coat was soaked, my patience was thinner than a dime-store photograph, and the whiskey in my gut felt like a faulty circuit board waiting to blow.

I wasn't looking for truth. Truth was a luxury item these days, reserved only for people who could afford to buy silence with it. I was looking for a thread, a loose end that might point me toward something concrete enough to make the headache stop throbbing behind my eyes. Someone had vanished—a dame named Lila Thorne, all silk and desperation, gone into the neon mouth of the harbor like she’d never breathed before.

The smoke in the bar was thick enough to chew, a wet curtain filtering out the single shaft of sickly yellow light that dared pierce the gloom. I slid onto a cracked leather stool at the far end, right next to a dame whose eyes looked too old for her face—they held the kind of weary resignation only seen in people who had paid too much rent and survived.

A man finally slid into the booth opposite me. He smelled like expensive cigarettes and desperation. His name was Mr. Sterling, or maybe it was "The Broker." It didn't matter. When you deal with men like him, names are just currency used to delay the inevitable punch.

He didn't sit; he unfolded himself across the worn velvet bench like a snake deciding where it wanted to strike. He ordered two fingers of rye and watched me over the rim of his glass, that practiced, knowing look in his eyes—the look of a man who knew exactly how much trouble I was, and why I couldn't stop digging anyway.

"You’re wet," he murmured, not as a question, but as a diagnosis. "The rain gets to everything eventually."

I took a drag off the cigarette cupped between my fingers, letting the smoke curl up into the sickly yellow ceiling fixture. "And you," I said, keeping my voice low, flat, and disinterested, "smell like secrets bought at auction."

He chuckled—a dry, rasping sound that tasted like dust. He slid a photograph across the sticky laminate tabletop. It was Lila Thorne. She looked exactly as she did in the missing persons reports: stunning, fragile, and utterly doomed.

"She wasn't lost," he whispered, leaning closer so his breath, smelling faintly of mint and deceit, brushed my ear. "That’s what you gotta understand about people like her. They don't wander off. They get *pulled*."

I kept my gaze steady, watching the minute twitch at the corner of his mouth—the only flaw in his polished facade. I knew he wasn't giving me facts; he was giving me a script. He wanted me to believe that her disappearance was a grand narrative, a tragic act orchestrated by fate or desire.

"Pulled how?" I asked, my voice barely rising above the hiss of the barman cleaning glass behind us.

Sterling rested his elbows on the table, folding his hands over them like a magician showing off an empty pocket. His eyes didn't blink. They were too attentive, too perfect, promising salvation while whispering poison.

"The things you think you know about her—the cheap love affairs, the debts, the weeping tears… they’re all just static," he purred, leaning in so close I could see the flicker of something genuinely dangerous behind his pupils. "She left because she found a way out of this city, Detective. And those who want to keep her *here*—they'll do anything to make you believe otherwise."

He was talking circles around me. He gave me enough cryptic nonsense to make my gut twist, painting a picture that defied logic while simultaneously confirming every bad instinct I possessed. Was he telling the truth? Was he playing me against someone else? Did *he* know where she went, and just wanted me chasing shadows so he could collect his fee?

I didn't answer with words. Instead, I tapped the ash from my cigarette into a tray that already held enough debris to choke a small animal. The neon sign outside—*Open Until Dawn*—cast its ugly red glow across the sweat on Sterling’s brow.

"You're telling me half of it," I said finally, not looking at him, but at the rain-streaked reflection in the dark glass. "And the other half... is what you want me to believe."

He smiled then. It was a perfect, oily, utterly insincere smile that promised both fortune and ruin. He picked up the photo of Lila Thorne and let it drift back toward me like an accusation.

"You always think the best stories have two halves, Detective," he whispered. "The truth is usually just one long lie."
